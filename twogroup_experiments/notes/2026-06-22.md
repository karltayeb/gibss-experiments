
**Two group SER**  We see a pretty striking improvement in the performance of the twogroup SER when we estimate the latent group assignment *locally* for each covariate. This is analogous to the improvement we see when estimating the JJ variational parameter locally for each covariate: there is an observation specific parameter. There is the choice of estimating a shared latent $q(z)$ or conditioning on the variable selected: $q(z | \gamma)$. 

**Cox vs cox reversed** Matthew observes that under a simple two group exponential arrival time model, the "reverse" method of modeling the most significant genes as arriving last is useful because it allows some early arrivals, whereas the "regular" approach of modeling the most significant genes as arriving first is not forgiving of even a single late arrival. Analysis of the score shows that a single late arrival decreases the gradient by $O(\log n)$ while and early arrival increases the gradient $O(1)$. 

If you consider an enrichment problem. The enriched gene sets are probably a mixture of null and non-null. One benefit of the reverse model is that the ranking distribution can be decomposed into a mixture of a null component plus an additional component. To respect the proportional hazards assumption

$X \sim \text{Exp}(1)$ and $Y \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda)$. Let $Z = F_X(Y)$ 
$$
Z(Y) = \mathbb{P}(X < Y | Y) = 1 - e^{-Y}, \quad F_{Z}(t) = \mathbb{P}(Z \le t) = \mathbb{P}(Y \le -\log(1 -t)) = 1 - (1-t)^\lambda
$$
Then in the case that $\lambda > 1$
$$
	f_{Z}(t) = \frac{d}{dt}F_{Z}(t) = \lambda (1 - t)^{\lambda-1} \iff Z \sim B(1, \lambda).
$$


**Mispecification** Naturally, fitting a model with the wrong covariates is mispecified. In Cox, fitting to x2 when x1 is the true covariate is mispecified. When $x_2$ and $x_{1}$ are correlated we typically see an attenuation of the effect. In the case of cox regression it works like this. Suppose $x_{1}$ and $x_{2}$ are binary covariates. $Y | X_1=1 \sim \exp(1)$, $Y | X_1=1 \sim \exp(\lambda_{1})$$Y | X_1=1 \sim \exp(\lambda_{1})$. Then $Y | X_2$ is a mixture of exponentials. Far out in the tail of observations times, only observations from the longer lived group survives, and so the hazard ratio tends to $1$. 

In cox (regular) we see an extreme mis-specification where for $|z| > 1$ the hazard ratio rapidly decays to 1. If gene sets were allowed to grow it becomes likely that a gene from the enriched group is null and appears deep in the ranking. Each such observation puts strong, negative downward pressure on the effect of an enriched gene set, leading to strong attenuation of the estimated effect. 

It feels like regular cox should return smaller credible sets, because the attenuation effect will be more extreme. 

**Mispecification or direction** We compare the performance of cox and cox reverse on gaussian twogroup simulations to enrichment and depletion in the well specified twogroup exponential arrival time model. While we do see the expected behavior that we are more sensitive to detect depletion than we are to detect enrichment. We can see that the gap between uncensored cox and uncensored cox-reverse is much larger that enrichment/depletion. The gap in performance is more comparable when you compare censored cox and uncensored cox reverse. I think this suggests that the mispecification of uncensored cox is a major (the hazard ratio is close to one for cox)
